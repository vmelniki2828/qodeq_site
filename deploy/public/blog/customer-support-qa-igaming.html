Why manual QA does not scale in casino support

Manual QA does not scale because the volume of iGaming support grows faster than any QA team can read. A typical setup is one or two QA specialists who pull a random sample of chats each week, read them against a checklist and record scores in a spreadsheet or helpdesk form.

That process covers roughly 10–15% of chats at best. Each review takes 3–5 minutes, and the fully loaded cost of a manually checked chat is around $0.90. In practice, on busy projects the coverage is much lower: the QA lead reads something like 2 chats out of 1,000.

The problem is not effort — it is statistics. Problems that cost you players are rare per chat but frequent in total:

  • a rude or dismissive reply to a player who has been waiting for a withdrawal for hours;
  • a wrong answer about wagering requirements or bonus terms that later turns into a complaint;
  • a missed escalation — a payment dispute, a fraud signal or a sensitive account case handled as routine;
  • an operator who ignores the knowledge base and improvises answers about limits or verification.

If such issues appear in a few percent of conversations, a 10–15% sample will catch some of them, but never with enough consistency to coach every operator. And the feedback usually arrives days or weeks after the chat — too late to matter.

What a support QA scorecard for iGaming should measure

A good iGaming QA scorecard measures both how the operator communicated and whether the answer was correct under your rules. Generic customer-service checklists focus on politeness; casino and sportsbook support also needs accuracy on payments, bonuses, KYC and compliance-sensitive topics.

How AI scoring of support chats works

AI QA reads every chat as it closes, scores it against the weighted criteria and links each finding to the exact line where it happened. The review takes about 5 seconds per chat instead of 3–5 minutes, so coverage stops being a budget decision.

  1. Chats arrive in real time. The system has read-only access to your chat history and picks up every closed conversation.
  2. Each chat is scored on 15 criteria. The model evaluates greeting, context, accuracy, tone, resolution and the rest of your scorecard.
  3. Breaches get timestamps. Every finding points to the specific line in the dialogue, with an improvement recommendation.
  4. QA managers filter and drill down. Search by operator, shift, presence of escalations and other parameters to find patterns, not just single mistakes.
  5. Humans re-check and correct. In the manual re-check module, QA managers review chats anonymously, adjust AI scores and leave coaching comments — and those corrections keep training the algorithm.

Scoring accuracy is 97.6%. Because every score is tied to specific lines in the chat, a supervisor can open any result and see exactly why points were deducted — the score is auditable, not a black box. The system has already checked 1.5 million chats on live projects.

The goal is not to replace QA judgement but to apply it everywhere. The AI does the reading; your QA team decides what “good” means, calibrates the model and spends its time on coaching.

Full coverage also changes what QA can answer. With a sample you can say whether an operator is “roughly fine”; with every chat scored you can see how quality moves by shift, by market language, by topic and around specific events such as a tournament weekend or a payment provider outage. Patterns that looked like individual mistakes often turn out to be gaps in the knowledge base or a script that no longer matches your rules.

From scores to coaching: per-operator reports

Scores only matter if they change how operators work, so AI QA turns them into daily and monthly reports for each operator. A typical report shows the average score, number of chats handled, and the top three strengths and development areas.

This changes the rhythm of coaching. Instead of a monthly review based on a handful of chats, a team lead can see on the same day that an operator keeps skipping context lookup on withdrawal questions, open the exact lines, and fix the habit before it reaches hundreds of players. Teams that work this way lift performance by 25–40%.

  • Daily view: who dropped below target yesterday and on which criteria.
  • Shift view: whether night shifts or peak days (tournaments, big matches, promos) degrade quality.
  • Escalation view: chats where an escalation happened or should have happened.
  • Monthly view: trends per operator and per team for performance reviews.
